*Prana* means breath, or life force; *ayama* means to extend or control it. Together, pranayama names a family of breathing techniques thought to date back to roughly 5000 BCE, recorded in texts like Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, where it appears as the fourth of eight limbs of yoga — the stage meant to prepare mind and body for meditation itself, not a warm-up to be rushed past.

## What the practice actually involves

Pranayama isn't one technique but a family: some variations slow the breath down, some hold it, some alternate it between nostrils, each aimed at a slightly different physiological effect. What unites them is the premise that breath is the most direct lever available for changing the state of the nervous system — faster to influence than thought, and easier to observe than emotion.

## What recent research adds

The tradition's claims have started to accumulate modern evidence. A 2024 study found four weeks of regular pranayama practice measurably lowered perceived stress and improved psychological quality of life. Separate research links it to improved sleep quality and reduced snoring in people with obstructive sleep apnea, lower systolic blood pressure, better lung function in athletes, and — across a 2020 review of 18 controlled trials — real symptomatic benefit for people with chronic respiratory illness, including COPD.

None of this makes pranayama a substitute for medical treatment. It does make it one of the few meditation-adjacent practices with a genuine, growing clinical literature behind it.
