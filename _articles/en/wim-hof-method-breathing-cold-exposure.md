Wim Hof, the Dutch athlete and speaker known publicly as the Iceman, was born on 20 April 1959. Guinness World Records, which keeps a page on him, dates his interest in extreme cold to his teenage years: he would sit out in the snow for hours at a stretch and sleep outside through the winter with neither tent nor sleeping bag, and came to believe he could stay warm in conditions where that should not have been possible. Guinness credits him with 18 record titles. He still holds the mark for the fastest half marathon run barefoot on ice or snow, set in 2007 at 2 hr 16 min 34 sec; he twice took the record for the longest swim under ice on a single breath, with 50 m in 1999 and 57.5 m in 2000, and took the longest-full-body-contact-with-ice title fifteen separate times, peaking in 2013 at 1 hr 53 min 2 sec. Both of those last two records have since passed to other holders.

The same account places the turn in his life after his wife took her own life, leaving him to raise four children alone. He fell into a deep depression, and it was then, Guinness reports, that he began swimming in ice-cold rivers to dull the pain — the start of what became decades of self-experimentation. Around that history he built a teaching system, the Wim Hof Method, run through Innerfire BV, the company he operates with his son Enahm, and taught through courses and a network of instructors he certifies himself.

## What a practitioner actually does

The organization describes the method as three linked components, and Hof's own materials are specific about what each one involves.

The breathing exercise, as wimhofmethod.com sets it out, begins seated or lying down in a relaxed position. The practitioner takes around thirty consecutive deep breaths — in fully through the nose or mouth, out passively, without forcing the exhale — then, after the final exhale, holds the breath until the urge to breathe returns. One large recovery breath follows, held for fifteen seconds and released. That sequence makes up a single round, and the site recommends three to four of them. The pattern is a cyclic one of hyperventilation followed by breath retention, not a single held breath.

Cold exposure is introduced gradually. The organization's own guidance starts a newcomer with a thirty-second cold shower at the end of an ordinary one and has them lengthen it from there; its cold-therapy material covers both cold showers and ice baths. The point of the sequencing, as the site puts it, is to let the body adapt under stress rather than to begin with prolonged immersion.

The third component, which the organization calls "mindset" or commitment, is presented as training in attention rather than a separate exercise: staying with a breath-hold, a cold shower or another point of discomfort past the first urge to stop it, and directing attention deliberately during that discomfort instead of letting it wander or panic. The organization frames this as the connective piece — the same attentional discipline used in the breathing and the cold is meant to carry over into how a person responds to stress more generally.

## What the method is said to produce

Hof and his organization make broad claims for the combination. Their own science page runs well past inflammation and immune response: reduced depressive symptoms, lower anxiety, stress management, mental clarity, pain perception and chronic pain, improved sleep, energy, lung function and glucose control, and a list of named conditions — multiple sclerosis, psoriasis, axial spondyloarthritis, spinal cord injury, acute mountain sickness. The same page presents the 2025 University of Queensland trial as a head-to-head against mindfulness meditation on stress reduction, energy and mental clarity, from which, it says, the method emerged as the clear favourite. Those are the claims as the organization frames them, in its own language, on its own site — not an independent assessment of what has been established.

## The one study almost everything else refers back to

The most-cited evidence is a 2014 trial published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, led by Matthijs Kox and Peter Pickkers at Radboud University Medical Center. The authors declared no conflict of interest, and the work was funded by a grant from Reumafonds, the Dutch rheumatism foundation; the ten-day training itself, however, was delivered by Hof in person together with three instructors he had previously certified. Thirty healthy male volunteers were enrolled, and twenty-four went through the endotoxin experiment — twelve randomized to the training, twelve to none. The trained arm covered breathing exercises, cold exposure including ice-water immersion and bare-chested hiking in sub-zero temperatures, and a meditation component. All twenty-four were then given an intravenous injection of bacterial endotoxin, a standard, tightly dosed way of provoking a predictable immune response in a laboratory.

Before the endotoxin challenge began, plasma epinephrine in the trained group was already significantly above the untrained group's — 1.02 against 0.35 nanomoles per litre. It climbed further once the men began the breathing exercises, peaking at 2.08 nmol/L just before the injection, with individual subjects reaching as high as 5.3. Measured an hour after the endotoxin injection, the trained group's anti-inflammatory IL-10 was 194% higher than in untrained controls, while the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-alpha, IL-6 and IL-8 were, respectively, 53%, 57% and 51% lower. Flu-like symptoms — the standard way researchers gauge how unwell endotoxin makes a subject feel — peaked 56% lower in the trained group. The paper's stated conclusion was that voluntarily activating the sympathetic nervous system releases epinephrine and, through it, suppresses the innate immune response in living human subjects. The significance the authors attached to that rests on their own opening premise: that the autonomic nervous system and the innate immune system are both regarded as systems a person cannot voluntarily influence.

The same authors were candid about what the design leaves unresolved. It did not allow them to isolate which element of the breathing exercises produced the epinephrine rise, and they could not rule out that other parts of the training contributed — singling out the extreme cold and the rewarming that followed it, which they suggested might have amounted to a form of ischaemic preconditioning. How long the training needs to be, and how long its effects persist afterwards, they wrote, remained to be determined. They also noted that the result came from an acute inflammatory challenge in healthy volunteers, and that whether it extends to patients with chronic autoimmune disease was an open question.

## What later, independent trials found

Later research, none of it authored by Hof or his organization, has produced a mixed record.

A 2024 systematic review by Omar Almahayni and Lucy Hammond at Warwick Medical School pooled nine papers covering eight trials published between 2014 and July 2022. Its verdict on the underlying literature was blunt: the quality of the studies, it said, is very low, and every result must therefore be read with caution. Samples ran from fifteen to forty-eight people and were 86.4% male, and almost every trial was judged of high concern for bias — largely because participants cannot be kept unaware of whether they are taking ice baths, which the review treats as a limitation built into the intervention rather than a failing of the researchers. Its stated conclusion was that the method "may produce promising immunomodulatory effects," with more and better research needed to substantiate that. On stress, anxiety, depression and pain the review found the literature simply had not yet examined most of the claims being made.

Two more recent controlled trials reached different conclusions again, on cardiovascular and on mental-health outcomes. A University of Bern team led by Sascha Ketelhut recruited forty-two healthy young men, forty-one of whom completed the study — twenty-one practising the full method daily for fifteen days, twenty carrying on with their normal routine. Neither the cardiovascular measures (heart rate variability, blood pressure, pulse wave velocity as an index of arterial stiffness) nor the psychological ones (perceived stress, affect, subjective vitality) separated the two groups. The authors' stated conclusion was that the method "should be questioned as a complementary therapy in cardiovascular prevention." A separate randomized trial led by Robin Blades at UCLA and Elissa Epel at UCSF, with collaborators at several other US universities, put eighty-four midlife women with high stress and depressive symptoms through three weeks of daily fifteen-minute audio-guided sessions: either the Wim Hof Method or an active control of slow-paced breathing at eight breaths a minute followed by a warm shower. Both arms improved by about a quarter on depressive symptoms, with gains still present at three months, and the authors wrote that "counter to the preregistered predictions," the two produced equivalent reductions.

A larger 2025 trial from the University of Queensland, by Nadia Fox, Hannah Biddell and Jemma King, followed 404 adults through a 29-day intervention: the Wim Hof Method delivered either in person or remotely, against a meditation control. It is the trial the organization now cites most, and on the measures it highlights the finding holds — the Wim Hof arms reported greater momentary gains in energy, mental clarity and ability to handle stress than the meditation arm, and the gap widened across the month. The trial measured a good deal more than that, though, and not all of it ran the same way: meditation came out ahead on trait anxiety and on sleep duration, and while the Wim Hof arms were faster on executive-function tasks, the meditation group was more accurate. The design is semi-randomized, with participants assigned by location rather than at random throughout, and its headline measures are momentary self-reports rather than blood or physiological markers. Its funding and competing-interest declarations could not be confirmed from the sources consulted here. It stands as the largest single trial to report an advantage for the method over an active comparison — not as a replication of the 2014 endotoxin result.

## The breathing exercise and its risks

The organization's own instructions carry an explicit warning: performed intensely across multiple rounds, the breathing exercise "can in rare cases lead to loss of consciousness." The site's own framing of that risk is worth stating in full, because it draws a distinction the warning depends on — fainting is, in its words, physiologically harmless in itself, and the danger lies entirely in where it happens. Hence the accompanying guidance: practise only seated or lying down, in a neutral indoor space, never in or near water and never at the wheel.

That warning exists because the technique's mechanism — rapid breathing that lowers blood carbon dioxide, followed by a breath-hold — can suppress the body's urge to breathe well before oxygen actually runs low, so that a person can lose consciousness with no warning sensation at all. In water, that combination produces what specialists call shallow-water blackout, a documented cause of drowning independent of any inability to swim.

On 10 August 2022, Raphael Metzger found his seventeen-year-old daughter, Madelyn Rose, dead in the shallow pool behind the family house in California. His account is that she had been performing Hof's breathing exercises, lost consciousness and drowned. He brought a civil claim for damages — 62 million euros — against Wim Hof and his son Enahm, who run Innerfire BV together. Because it was a civil action, no criminal penalty was at stake.

The Hofs' defence was not a bare denial. They argued that their materials consistently instruct practitioners to do the exercises sitting or lying on the ground and to keep away from anywhere unsafe, water and bicycles included. In July 2024 a Californian court ruled against the claim. The reasoning reaching the press came through Hof's lawyer, Robert Matsuishi, who told the Dutch daily de Volkskrant that the court had found no indication that Madelyn Metzger performed the breathing exercises in the pool that day, or that those exercises actually caused her to drown. The distinction matters and is easy to lose: the ruling turned on causation not being established, not on any finding that the technique is safe. Her father's lawyer said he would appeal.

The journalist Scott Carney has separately kept a running tally of deaths and injuries reported in connection with the breathing exercises. His position is an unusual one. He first met Hof in 2013 intending to expose him, became a convert instead, wrote *What Doesn't Kill Us* about the experience, promoted the method across hundreds of media appearances, and earned affiliate commissions of $5,324 from Innerfire between 2017 and 2021 — a relationship he now discloses himself as part of turning sharply critical of the organization's safety messaging. His count, last updated on 1 January 2024, stands at 21 deaths and 18 injuries, twelve of the deaths in the United States. Of the nineteen cases he examined in detail, thirteen were drownings of practitioners he judged to have likely lost consciousness, with the remaining six comprising cardiac arrests in ice water and people who drowned but survived. He compiled it from news reports, police and coroners' records, and tips from within the Wim Hof community and from drowning-watchdog organizations. It is a journalist's own compilation rather than an official statistic, and it has not been independently verified here — though the physiological mechanism underlying it, hypocapnia suppressing the urge to breathe, is well established in the wider medical literature on breath-hold diving and hyperventilation.

## What the record shows, and what remains the organization's own claim

The strongest evidence behind the Wim Hof Method sits in one place: a single 2014 trial showing that ten days of combined training let twelve young men blunt a laboratory-induced inflammatory response through a measurable and specific mechanism — voluntary sympathetic activation and an epinephrine surge. It is the study the rest of the case rests on, and it was authored by a research team declaring no interest in the outcome.

Everything built outward from it is less settled. Broader claims about mood, chronic disease and general immune-system benefit rest on a body of trials a systematic review has judged very low in quality, and on more recent controlled studies pointing in different directions — one finding no cardiovascular effect, another finding the method no better than an active control for depression, and a third, larger trial finding a self-reported advantage over meditation on some measures while the meditation comparison came out ahead on others. The breathing technique's own combination of hyperventilation and breath-holding carries a specific, mechanistically explained risk that the organization's instructions flag themselves, and that has been the subject of a civil damages claim over the drowning of a seventeen-year-old — decided in Hof's favour on the question of causation, not on the safety of the technique, and under appeal. What a decade of trials has produced is a narrow, well-supported physiological finding surrounded by a much larger set of claims still waiting on the kind of evidence that supports it.
