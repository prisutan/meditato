*This article is historical and cultural in nature. It does not describe, recommend, or provide instructions for ayahuasca use, which involves serious physical and psychological risk and is illegal in many countries outside its traditional context.*

Ceremonies built around ayahuasca — a brew combining a vine and a leaf, prepared according to knowledge passed down for generations — are woven into daily life across Amazonian indigenous societies as routinely as hunting or gathering, rather than set apart as rare, special occasions. Archaeological evidence, including a ceremonial cup found in Ecuador, suggests the practice may be over 2,500 years old.

## What the ceremony is understood to do

Among peoples including the Huni Kuin, Yawanawá, Ashaninka, Tukano, and Yanomami, these ceremonies function as a communal diagnostic space: a setting where physical and mental ailments are examined, and where ancestors can be consulted for guidance. The shaman's role here is distinct from a Western religious leader — described in anthropological literature as a "traveler between worlds," trained through fasting, sustained observation, and dreams rather than formal instruction.

## Why this matters for understanding meditation broadly

Amazonian indigenous spirituality doesn't separate contemplative practice from ordinary life the way many other traditions do — working, planting, healing, and ritual are treated as points on a single continuum, not as separate categories. Understanding ayahuasca ceremonies in that context, rather than through the lens of Western psychedelic tourism, is essential to understanding what the practice actually means to the communities that have sustained it.
