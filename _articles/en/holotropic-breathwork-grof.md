Stanislav Grof spent the early part of his career studying LSD's effects on the psyche, building much of the theoretical foundation for what became pre- and perinatal psychology. When LSD research was legally shut down in the late 1960s, Grof didn't abandon the states of consciousness he'd been studying — he looked for another way in.

## Breath as the substitute

At the Esalen Institute in Big Sur, California, where he became scholar-in-residence in 1973, Grof and his future wife Christina developed Holotropic Breathwork: accelerated breathing combined with evocative music and supportive bodywork, conducted in a safe group setting, as a substance-free route to the same non-ordinary states of consciousness he'd previously accessed with psychedelics. The name itself — from the Greek *holos* (whole) and *trepein* (moving toward) — describes the intent: moving toward wholeness, not away from ordinary awareness for its own sake.

## What the technique claims to surface

Practitioners report experiences ranging from vivid childhood and infancy memories to material framed as prenatal or birth-related, alongside imagery drawn from what Grof's framework calls the historical and archetypal layers of the collective unconscious. These claims sit well outside mainstream clinical psychology's usual evidentiary standards, and should be read as a description of the tradition's own framework rather than an independently verified mechanism.

What's verifiable is the lineage: a serious clinical researcher, blocked from his original tool, engineered breath alone to reach a comparable altered state — a direct link between 1960s psychedelic research and a breathwork practice still taught today.
