Zazen — seated Zen meditation — asks almost nothing of the practitioner beyond posture, breath, and a willingness to watch thoughts pass without chasing them. It has been taught in Japanese temples for centuries, but its influence runs well past the meditation hall: it shapes how gardens are laid out, how tea is served, how a room is built to hold silence.

Shinrin-yoku, "forest bathing," is a newer name for an old instinct. The term was coined in 1982 by Japan's Forest Agency, framing a walk through the woods as a deliberate physiological and psychological practice rather than ordinary exercise. It draws on Shinto and Buddhist reverence for nature as something already whole, not a backdrop to be admired from a trail.

## Two practices, one method

Zazen and shinrin-yoku share a method more than a location: both ask you to slow down enough that attention itself becomes the point, not a means to somewhere else. Forest bathing has been measured to lower cortisol and blood pressure and to increase natural-killer-cell activity in the immune system — Zen without a cushion, as some practitioners describe it, trading the meditation hall for a denser field of bark, light, and moving air.

## Trying it without ceremony

For a beginner, the practical version needs no ritual: a slow, unhurried walk with no destination, senses open rather than narrated to yourself. What zazen does through stillness, a forest can do through simple, unstructured attention.
