In the late 1970s, Jon Kabat-Zinn built an eight-week program at the University of Massachusetts Medical School with a deliberately clinical, secular framing — stripped of Buddhist terminology, aimed at chronic pain and stress patients rather than spiritual seekers. That program, Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), is the direct ancestor of most of what gets called "mindfulness" in hospitals, apps, and workplaces today.

## The technique itself

The body scan is deceptively simple: lying down, attention moves sequentially through each part of the body, noting physical sensations — along with whatever cognitive or emotional reactions arise in response — without trying to change any of it. It's one of three formal techniques taught in MBSR, alongside seated mindfulness meditation and simple yoga postures, supported by weekly two-hour group sessions, a six-hour retreat day, and 45 minutes of daily homework across the eight weeks.

## What the evidence says now

A systematic review and meta-analysis of body scan research found consistent, measurable reductions in depression, anxiety, pain, and stress after the full eight-week course — one of the more replicated findings in contemplative research generally, likely because MBSR's manualized structure makes it far easier to study consistently than looser, self-directed practice.

The body scan's real innovation wasn't the technique — attention to body sensation has ancient roots — but the packaging: a fixed, secular, clinically testable protocol that hospitals and insurers could actually adopt.
