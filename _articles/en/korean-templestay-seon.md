Korean Buddhism has one habit that sets it apart from many neighboring traditions: it distrusts the idea that reading about enlightenment gets you any closer to it. Seon (the Korean form of Zen) insists on direct experience over scriptural study — a stance it has held for roughly 1,700 years.

## What the monastics actually do

Twice a year, in summer and winter, around 2,500 monastics enter a 100-day meditation retreat across roughly 100 temples nationwide — a uniquely Korean institution within Mahayana Buddhism. The core practice is *ganhwa seon*, a form of meditation built around sitting with a single, unresolvable question rather than working through a chain of logical steps.

## Templestay: the version anyone can try

Since 2002, the Jogye Order has run Templestay, an overnight program at working temples open to visitors with no Buddhist background. Guests trade their clothes for gray robes, eat in stacked lacquered bowls alongside monks and nuns, and join *chamseon* (seated meditation), dawn chanting, and the 108 prostrations — a repeated bowing ritual meant to symbolically release accumulated defilements one at a time.

It is, in effect, a controlled, short-term dose of a tradition that otherwise asks for a hundred days at a stretch — a way to feel the shape of Seon practice without committing a season of your life to it.
