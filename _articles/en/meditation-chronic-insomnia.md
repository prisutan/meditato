Chronic insomnia is stubborn precisely because trying harder to sleep tends to make it worse — which is part of why mindfulness-based approaches have drawn sustained research interest: they don't ask the person to force anything, only to notice.

## What the recent evidence converges on

A 2025 scoping review found mindfulness meditation significantly improved sleep quality and reduced insomnia severity, with one consistent pattern across the studies it covered: interventions combining structured, face-to-face sessions with at-home practice outperformed either approach alone. Purely app-based or purely in-person programs both did less well than the hybrid.

## What's still being tested

Several active trials are now trying to isolate which component actually matters. One ongoing study assigns participants to one of three arms — guided bedtime meditation, unguided daytime meditation, or sleep-hygiene education alone — specifically to separate the effect of *meditating* from the effect of simply *learning about* sleep. Recruitment for a related telemedicine-based trial is scheduled through 2026.

Separately, researchers have started asking a more unusual question: whether long-term meditators show a measurably younger "sleep-based brain age" than non-meditators, using EEG-derived aging markers rather than self-reported sleep quality. Early findings suggest they do — a hint that the benefits of sustained practice may show up in sleep architecture itself, not just in how rested someone feels the next morning.
