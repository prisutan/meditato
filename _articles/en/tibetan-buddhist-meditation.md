Buddhist contemplative practice has been documented in Tibet since at least the 7th century CE, and over that time it absorbed an unusually wide range of Indian and Central Asian traditions — wider than almost any other Buddhist lineage. The result is a system that includes standard Mahayana meditation forms, but also several practices found nowhere else.

## Beyond the meditation cushion

Deity Yoga, in which a practitioner visualizes themselves as an enlightened figure in precise detail, is the tradition's defining technique. Alongside it sit Mahamudra and Dzogchen — taught respectively by the Kagyu and Nyingma schools — both aimed at directly recognizing the nature of mind rather than reasoning one's way toward it. More unusual still are Dream Yoga, Tummo (a technique for generating internal heat), practices tied to the Bardo — the intermediate state between death and rebirth — and Chöd, a confrontational visualization practice.

Before any of this, practitioners typically complete the *ngöndro*: preliminary practices built on visualization, mantra recitation, and large numbers of prostrations, meant to prepare the mind for what follows. Periodically, communities also hold a *drubchen* — an intensive group retreat lasting about ten days, led by a senior lama and open to both monastics and laypeople.

Tantric technique is one of the few through-lines that mark Tibetan Buddhism as distinct even among other Buddhist meditation traditions — a system built less around calming down and more around directly reshaping how the mind perceives itself.
