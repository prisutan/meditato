For most of its history, vipassana meditation was presumed to be a monastic pursuit — something a layperson supported from the outside but didn't practice directly. That assumption broke down in Burma in the mid-20th century, and one monk in particular is credited with breaking it.

## From revival to method

The shift started earlier, with Ledi Sayadaw's late-19th-century revival of vipassana practice based on close textual study combined with his own meditative experience. That revival fed into the "New Burmese Method" developed by the monk U Narada (Mingun Sayadaw) in the early 20th century — whose most influential student, Mahasi Sayadaw (1904–1982), turned it into something teachable at scale: a systematized technique built around continuous, moment-to-moment awareness of the body and mind, centered on a single, simple anchor — the sensation of the belly rising and falling with each breath.

## Letting laypeople in the door

When Burma's prime minister, U Nu, went looking for a teacher to run a meditation center open to ordinary people in Rangoon, it was Mahasi Sayadaw's method that filled that role — deliberately taught by lay instructors to lay students, a genuine departure from monastic-only practice. The approach spread from there to Sri Lanka and Thailand, and eventually to the West through students like Joseph Goldstein and Jack Kornfield, who carried the "noting" technique — silently labeling rising, falling, thinking, hearing — into what much of the modern mindfulness movement now takes for granted.
