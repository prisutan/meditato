*This article describes a spiritual practice sacred to specific Indigenous nations. It is presented here as historical and cultural context, not as instruction or invitation to practice outside that context.*

Across many Indigenous nations of North America, the vision quest represents one of the most demanding contemplative traditions on record: a solitary period of fasting, isolation, and sustained prayer, undertaken specifically to invite a vision from a guardian spirit.

## What the practice involves

A seeker — traditionally most often, though not exclusively, a young man — withdraws alone into a remote natural setting for several days, fasting and praying while humbling themselves before what is often described as the Great Mystery. The goal is direct spiritual insight: guidance toward a life purpose, a protective guardian spirit, or a clearer sense of one's place within the larger cosmos — arrived at through physical hardship and isolation rather than guided instruction.

## Not one tradition, but many

It's important not to flatten this into a single, uniform ritual. The vision quest takes different forms, with different specific rules and interpretations, across the 574 federally recognized tribal nations in the United States alone — a diversity that reflects genuinely distinct spiritual systems, not regional variations on one theme.

## A practice that survived active suppression

Christian missionaries actively discouraged the vision quest, and colonial governments outlawed versions of it during the 19th and 20th centuries. Despite that, it remained an important living practice for many Indigenous communities into the 21st century — a continuity that says as much about cultural resilience as it does about the practice itself.
