The Thai Forest Tradition began as a reform, not an innovation. In the early 20th century, the monk Ajahn Mun Bhuridatto led a return to the Buddha's original teachings after centuries of ritual and textual accretion — moving practice out of city temples and back into the forest, where it had started.

## The technique

Its core tool is *anapanasati*, mindfulness of breathing, often paired with the silent mental repetition of "Buddho" — a word used less as a prayer than as an anchor, something to hold attention steady while the breath does the actual work of settling the mind. Around this center, forest monks also teach contemplation of the body and of death, loving-kindness, and walking meditation, adapting the emphasis to the individual student rather than applying one fixed sequence to everyone.

## Calm and insight, not calm or insight

Ajahn Chah, one of the tradition's most influential teachers, described *samatha* (tranquility) and *vipassana* (insight) as "two sides of one hand" — inseparable, not sequential stages to move through. A forest monk deciding what to teach a new student isn't choosing between calming the mind and understanding it; the tradition treats those as the same motion, viewed from two sides.
