On a humid, overcast morning before Colombia's rainy season, a Kogi mama named Shibulata breaks off mid-conversation to announce that it is *nebshizha* — a cultivation period marked by the new moon — and that he must go and collect the village's confessions. Over the following days, families will bring him small twists of raw cotton, each one already carrying a specific worry, memory, or dream, which he will convert into a set of payments meant to call the rain and keep the sun from scorching the crops.

That scene, recorded by the anthropologist Falk Parra Witte during fieldwork among the Kogi, describes a practice with no real equivalent in the meditation traditions most outsiders know. Among the Kogi, and the related Arhuaco (Ika), Wiwa, and Kankuamo peoples who share Colombia's Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta, a specific thought can be moved out of a person and into an object, carried to a spiritual authority, and finally left at a designated site in the landscape — not to calm the mind, but to settle a debt.

## A debt owed to the mountain

The practice has two linked names. *Aluna ishguashi* — rendered in Spanish as *confesión*, "confession" — is the act of mentally discharging a thought, feeling, dream, or memory into a small object, most often cotton. *Zʉbihi*, or *pagamento* ("payment"), is the wider category of exchange it belongs to: offerings ranging from cotton, leaves, shells, crystals, seeds, and stone-beads to songs and dances, all directed at repaying what the Sierra Nevada continually provides.

The logic behind both is cosmological rather than therapeutic. In Kogi thought, as Parra Witte records it, the Great Mother — "the origin of all things" — conceived the world in a thought she called Aluna, and everything within it, from rivers to lineages to individual people, remains bound together by what his informants describe as threads of "truth and energy." Taking anything from that fabric — food, water, timber, or simply the right to exist in a particular valley — creates an obligation to give something back. Left unpaid, that obligation becomes *shalá*, debt, understood to surface as drought, sickness, or failed crops: not a punishment for wrongdoing, but evidence that the wider system has fallen out of balance and needs restoring.

## What the mamos do with what they are given

A person confessing holds bits of raw cotton "held between two fingers" and directs a specific piece of inner material into it — a recurring worry, the details of a dispute, a dream that needs interpreting. Once returned to a mama, he "gently shakes the cotton" while mentally addressing what has been placed there, then spiritually sends it toward the appropriate recipient — of water, of a particular crop, of a lineage — before physically depositing the object at a sacred site tied to that purpose.

Deciding exactly what should be paid, where, and to which spiritual authority is itself a formal process. Mamas perform water divination, dropping small stone beads into a bowl and reading the resulting patterns as answers from Aluna about which payment a given problem requires. Confessions collected from a village during a single *nebshizha* — as with Shibulata's, above — are converted through that reading into a coordinated set of payments meant to call rain, protect crops, or settle a dispute.

The practice is not reserved for specialists, and its life-shaping moments are formalized rather than left to individual habit: Parra Witte describes confession-based payments used to "name" a newborn and tie it to the physical world, and similar payments mark adulthood and marriage. Among the Arhuaco specifically, National Geographic reports that children are taught from an early age to fill everyday materials — wool, cotton, string — with their own reflections about the natural world, folding the technique into ordinary upbringing rather than treating it as a discrete rite performed once.

## A shared system across four peoples

The Kogi, Arhuaco (Ika), Wiwa, and Kankuamo speak related Chibchan languages and each maintain a version of this confession-and-payment system, adapted to local vocabulary and material. In Parra Witte's account, the Ika term for it, *gʉn'gawʉn*, is said to denote "feeding, cleansing, and the renewal of relationships." In Arhuaco villages such as Sogrome, mamos have been documented sitting at fixed stone seats — said to be "antennas to communicate with the sky," National Geographic reported — to think through how best to protect the surrounding land, before carrying colored paper or stones charged with community reflections down the mountainside to specific deposit points. Some Arhuaco offerings travel much farther: a twelve-day pilgrimage climbs to Lake Naboba, nearly 16,000 feet up, where cotton fiber and stones — white for snow and ice, green for the coca leaf chewed by the men, gray for water — are given to the lake itself. Twenty-five-year-old Ever Maestre put the logic to National Geographic in plainly economic terms: the offerings are "our way of paying our bills to the mountain and the Earth."

Despite this variation in vocabulary and material, the underlying structure recurs across all four groups: a network of named sacred sites spanning the Sierra Nevada, spiritual authorities trained to mediate between people and those sites, and a shared conviction that receiving anything from the land creates an obligation that must be actively, repeatedly discharged rather than assumed away.

## UNESCO's 2022 recognition

In 2022, UNESCO inscribed the "Ancestral System of Knowledge of the Four Indigenous Peoples — Arhuaco, Kankuamo, Kogui, and Wiwa — of the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta" on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. The listing describes a body of sacred mandates, held and interpreted by mamos and sagas, that governs care for sacred sites, life-cycle rites, and what UNESCO's own summary terms "retributions or offerings to spiritual powers" — the payment system that includes confession among its component practices.

The recognition does not single out cotton-twist confessions as a standalone tradition; it protects the wider framework of knowledge, relationships to specific mountain sites, and generational transmission that makes practices like *aluna ishguashi* and *zʉbihi* possible at all. For the Four Peoples, whose sacred territory has repeatedly come under pressure from settlement, mining, and tourism along the Sierra Nevada's base, that framework — and the everyday discipline of paying what is owed to it — is presented as inseparable from the survival of the mountain itself.
