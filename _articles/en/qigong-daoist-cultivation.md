Qigong's roots reach back further than most people expect — Daoist antecedents date to roughly 2146 BCE, and the broader practice draws on more than 4,000 years of Chinese thought about breath, movement, and vitality. What's sold today as a single discipline is really an accretion: *xingqi* ("circulating qi"), *daoyin* ("guiding and pulling"), plus threads from the I Ching, Confucian and Buddhist philosophy, traditional Chinese medicine, and martial arts. The term "qigong" itself — "cultivating the qi" — only became standard in the mid-20th century.

## What it looks like in practice

The *Zhuangzi* already describes exercises modeled on animal movement paired with specific breathing patterns, aimed at longevity and recovery from illness. By the Song dynasty (960–1279), one form — the Eight Pieces of Brocade (*Ba Duan Jin*) — had become popular enough that it's still widely practiced today: slow, deliberate movement combined with breath and focused attention, meant to open the body's meridians and let qi move freely.

## The medical framing

In traditional Chinese medicine, each internal organ is linked to a specific meridian, and qigong is understood as a way of influencing organ health by working on those channels rather than the organs directly — a system-level intervention rather than a symptom-level one.
