Japan's shinrin-yoku inspired a practice, not a franchise — but in 2012, psychologist Amos Clifford built a structured, certifiable version of it for a Western audience, and that version has since spread further than the original term ever did on its own.

## What changed in translation

The Association of Nature and Forest Therapy Guides and Programs (ANFT) uses forest bathing as its foundation but adds a trauma-informed, relational framework explicitly designed around the guide-participant relationship, not just the walk itself. Guide training draws on current medical research and nature-connection science alongside the older mindfulness traditions it's built from. The core principles are specific: open all the senses to the land, measure the walk by time spent rather than distance covered, stay fully present to allow for healing, and commit to it as an ongoing practice rather than a one-time outing.

## How far it's spread

ANFT has now trained more than 2,000 guides operating in over 60 countries, including certification programs across the US, Canada, Europe, Australia, New Zealand, and Central and South America — a scale Japan's original practice never had a formal mechanism to reach on its own. A review compiling 17 separate studies found a consistent stress-reducing effect of nature exposure across body systems, and forest therapy programs are increasingly used in trauma-informed community mental health work, including in disaster-affected areas.
